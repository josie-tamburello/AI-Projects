# What to look for when buying balsamic vinegar

1. **Check for the right certification:** For traditional balsamic, look for DOP. That regime means only cooked grape must (no wine vinegar as the base), from authorised grape varieties (e.g. Lambrusco and Trebbiano) grown only in the defined province. Anything else is a different category of product.

2. **Check the legal aging tiers (traditional only):** genuine balsamic vinegar is sold in only two maturity classes: white cap (minimum 12 years) and gold cap (minimum 25 years). There is no official “5-year” or “15-year” traditional grade between these; the liquid in the barrel is always a blend across years, so labels refer to minimum age, not a single vintage.

3. **Check the bottle:** authentic balsamic vinegar is bottled only in the consortium-approved 100 ml bottle (the Giugiaro design). Same shape and capacity for all certified producers; only the front label differs. Any other bottle shape or size is not that certified traditional product.

4. **Check “Balsamic Vinegar of Modena” vs traditional:** Most supermarket “Balsamico di Modena” is wine vinegar with caramel, sugar, thickeners and sometimes other additives, aged days to weeks in wood useful for everyday cooking, but not the same as the DOP. Read the ingredients and the category on the label.

5. **Check the label for honesty on age:** on industrial or decorative bottles, large numbers (e.g. “10”, “50”, “100”) often refer to travasi (refills) or marketing, not years of aging. Distrust age claims that don’t match the regulatory system above.

6. **Check “bottled in” vs “produced in”:** “Balsamico di Modena” as a name is not protected everywhere the way some other EU names are; oil or vinegar can be blended elsewhere and only bottled in Modena. For traceability, prefer wording that shows where it was made and what it contains, not just where it was bottled.

7. **Check thickness and ingredients:** Very thick, syrupy “balsamic” is often sugar and caramel (and thickeners), not decades of evaporation. Authentic DOP balsamic vinegar gains body from water loss over years in open barrels; industrial viscosity is often from formulation. Glazes and “balsamic creams” are usually sweetened, thickened products which is fine if you want a condiment, but not traditional vinegar.

8. **Check “white balsamic”:** traditionally produced balsamic darkens with aging and oxidation; “white balsamic” on the market is typically treated (e.g. colour/flavour adjusted). Treat it as a separate, commercial product, not  DOP.

9. **Check expiry dates in context:** Industrial balsamics with sugar can spoil or change sooner. Short “best before” can reflect that. High-acid traditional vinegar is very stable; Italian retail may still show a legal maximum date, but the product does not behave like a sugary glaze.
