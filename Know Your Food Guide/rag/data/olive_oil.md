# What to look for when buying olive oil

1. **Check the grade and extraction:** choose extra virgin olive oil, which must be extracted by mechanical means without chemical refining. Prefer wording such as cold extracted (modern, controlled process) over vague marketing on other oils; avoid relying on “pure olive oil” or lower grades for everyday use, as they are less strictly defined and easier to adulterate.

2. **Check acidity and freshness data:** where the label or producer gives free acidity, extra virgin must be below 0.8% by regulation; many discerning buyers aim around 0.5% or lower. If peroxide value or a harvest date is stated, favour lower peroxide and more recent harvest — both point to fresher, less oxidised oil.

3. **Check origin and labelling honesty:** prefer single country of origin and, where possible, single estate or named variety. Read whether the oil is produced in that country, not only bottled there.

4. **Check the bottle and storage fit:** buy dark glass where you can, keep the cap tight, and plan to store the bottle away from heat and strong light (for example, not beside the stove). After opening, treat it as a perishable, aim to finish within a few months (often quoted around six months), not years.

5. **Check clarity and sediment:** for a normal retail oil that should be stable, avoid bottles that look murky with suspicious residue unless you deliberately want a very fresh, unsettled style and will use it quickly. Sediment can carry off-flavours and speed rancidity if left in contact with the oil.

6. **Check colour last:** Greener oil does not mean fresher or better; quality is judged by taste and chemistry, not hue.

7. **Check price and plausibility:** if an oil is very cheap yet labelled extra virgin, or tastes thin and flavourless, be alert to blending with refined seed or olive oils. A fridge set (oil thickening when chilled) can be a rough heuristic but is not definitive.
